A constellation, in depth

A constellation is a shape only because we see it from one place. Its stars can be a hundred times farther apart in depth than they are across the sky. Here every star of a figure stands at its real distance, and you pick the way your eyes take the depth in: move the view, wiggle it, or put on a pair of glasses.

Nothing here is a diagram. The eye really does sit a little way from Earth, looking at the middle of the figure, and each star really is at its catalogued distance. The depth slider sets how far the eye is allowed to move: at full strength it roams about half the distance to the nearest star of the figure, which is why a figure with one close star swings so much more than one whose stars are all far off. A figure that barely moves at any setting is one whose stars really are at much the same distance.

Star positions, magnitudes, spectral types and parallax distances come from the Hipparcos catalogue (ESA 1997) via VizieR; the far distances are estimates, and a parallax too small to measure is reported as no distance rather than a large one, so such a star is left out of the figure here. Star figures are the ones the IAU publishes, drawn by Alan MacRobert for Sky & Telescope, used under CC BY 4.0; the machine-readable tracing is from d3-celestial (BSD-3-Clause).
